Orthogonality check

Treat each row of the table as a list of numbers. Multiply two rows column by column, weight each column by its class size g, and add. Different irreducible representations always give 0: they are orthogonal. A row with itself gives the order h. Pick Γ3N as the second row to see that the reduction formula is the same sum divided by h.

ΣR g(R) · χi(R) · χj(R) = h if i = j, 0 if i ≠ j

Symmetry & point groups

Symmetry element

#

A point, line or plane that a symmetry operation is carried out about: a rotation axis, a mirror plane, an inversion centre or an improper-rotation axis. One element can generate several operations. The C3 axis of ammonia gives both C3 and C32.

ExampleBesides the identity, water has three elements: a C2 axis and two mirror planes.

See it inWaterFind the point group

Symmetry operation

#

A rotation, reflection, inversion, or rotation followed by reflection, after which the molecule cannot be told apart from where it started. Each atom lands on its own position or on the position of an identical atom. Together, a molecule's operations form its point group.

ExampleWater has four operations: E, C2, σv(xz) and σv′(yz).

See it inWaterAmmonia

Identity E

#

The operation "do nothing". Every molecule has it, and every group must contain it (see group multiplication). All atoms are unmoved and each contributes 3, so χ3N(E) = 3N. In any representation, χ(E) is the dimension.

CarefulThe italic operation E is not the same thing as the Mulliken label E for a two-dimensional representation.

See it inWater

Proper rotation Cn

#

Rotation by 360°/n about an axis. Cnk means k turns in a row, and Cnn = E. An unmoved atom contributes 1 + 2 cos(360°/n) to χ(Γ3N).

ExampleWater has a C2 (180°) axis and ammonia a C3 (120°). Benzene has a C6 axis perpendicular to the ring and six C2 axes in the ring plane.

See it inWaterAmmoniaBenzene

Principal axis

#

The rotation axis with the highest n. By convention it is the z axis. It decides whether a mirror plane is σh or σv, and whether a representation is labelled A or B (see Mulliken symbols). If several C2 axes tie, as in D2h, which one is z is a convention you have to state.

ExampleC6 in benzene, C3 in ammonia, C5 through the two rings of ferrocene.

See it inBenzeneFerrocene

Mirror plane σ (σh, σv, σd)

#

Reflection through a plane. Reflecting twice gives E, and an unmoved atom contributes 1. The subscript says how the plane sits relative to the principal axis:

  • σh, horizontal: perpendicular to the principal axis.
  • σv, vertical: contains the principal axis.
  • σd, dihedral: contains the principal axis and bisects the angle between two C2 axes perpendicular to it.

ExampleBenzene's molecular plane is a σh. Allene's two mirror planes are σd. A planar molecule with no other symmetry, such as bromochloroethene, has only E and its molecular plane (point group Cs).

See it inWaterAlleneBromochloroethene

Inversion centre i

#

Inversion through a point sends every point (x, y, z) to (−x, −y, −z). Only an atom at the centre can be unmoved, and it contributes −3. A molecule with an inversion centre has g/u labels (see Mulliken symbols) and obeys the rule of mutual exclusion.

ExampleCO2 and benzene have an inversion centre. Water and ammonia do not.

See it inCarbon dioxideBenzene

Improper rotation Sn

#

Rotation by 360°/n followed by reflection through the plane perpendicular to that axis. S1 is the same as σ and S2 is the same as i. A molecule can have an Sn axis without having Cn or σh on their own. An unmoved atom contributes −1 + 2 cos(360°/n).

ExampleAllene has an S4 axis along C=C=C but no C4. Benzene has S6 and S3 along its C6 axis.

See it inAlleneFerroceneBenzene

Point group

#

The complete set of symmetry operations of a molecule. They form a mathematical group (see group multiplication). It is called a point group because one point, the centre of mass, stays fixed under every operation. Point groups are named with Schoenflies symbols such as C2v, C3v, D6h, D∞h, Td and Oh.

ExampleWater is C2v, ammonia C3v, benzene D6h, CO2 D∞h.

See it inFind the point groupWater

Order h

#

The number of symmetry operations in a group. In a character table, add up the class coefficients along the top row. h is the 1/h in the reduction formula, and it equals the sum of the squares of the dimensions of the irreducible representations.

ExampleC2v: h = 4. C3v: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6. D6h: 24. The linear groups C∞v and D∞h have infinite order.

See it inAnatomy aboveAmmonia

Class

#

A set of operations that the other operations of the group carry into one another. Formally, A and B are in the same class if B = X−1AX for some operation X in the group. Operations in a class always have the same character, so a character table has one column per class, with the class size g written in front. The number of classes equals the number of irreducible representations.

ExampleIn ammonia, a C3 turn carries each σv plane into the next, so the three planes form one class, written 3σv. C3 and C32 form another, written 2C3.

See it inAnatomy aboveAmmonia

Group multiplication & closure

#

"Multiplying" two operations means doing one after the other. AB means do B first, then A. A set of operations is a group if:

  • Closure: the product of any two members is also a member.
  • It contains the identity E.
  • Every operation has an inverse in the set, which undoes it.
  • Multiplication is associative: (AB)C = A(BC).

ExampleIn C2v, doing C2 and then σv(xz) has the same effect as σv′(yz): (x, y, z) → (−x, −y, z) → (−x, y, z).

Abelian group

#

A group in which every pair of operations commutes: AB = BA. Then every operation is a class of its own, every g = 1, and there are h irreducible representations, all one-dimensional. When all the characters are real, as in Cs, Ci, C2, C2v, C2h, D2 and D2h, the molecule has no degenerate vibrations. Cyclic groups such as C3, C3h and S4 are abelian too, but their complex-conjugate representations pair up into E, and those vibrations are degenerate.

ExampleCs, C2v, C2h, D2 and D2h are abelian. C3v is not: C3 followed by σv is a different reflection from σv followed by C3.

See it inWaterCarbon dioxideBiphenyl

Subgroup

#

A subset of a group's operations that is a group by itself. Its order divides the order of the parent group. Substituting an atom or distorting a molecule lowers its symmetry to a subgroup, and a correlation table shows how the labels change.

Example{E, C3, C32} is the subgroup C3 of C3v. The CO2 lesson does its bookkeeping in D2h, a subgroup of D∞h.

See it inCarbon dioxide

Linear molecules & infinite groups C∞v, D∞h

#

The axis of a linear molecule is a C∞ axis: rotation by any angle φ is a symmetry operation, and infinitely many σv planes contain the axis. Without an inversion centre the group is C∞v (HCl, HCN, OCS). With one it is D∞h (CO2, N2, HC≡CH), which also has infinitely many C2 axes perpendicular to the molecule.

The order is infinite, so you cannot use the reduction formula directly. Instead, work in a finite subgroup (C2v or D2h) and correlate back. Irreducible representations get Greek labels, Σ+, Σ−, Π, Δ, with g/u added in D∞h. A linear molecule has 3N − 5 vibrations.

See it inCarbon dioxideDioxygen

Representations & character tables

Character table

#

A summary of a point group. It has one column per class of operations and one row per irreducible representation, with the characters in the body. Two columns on the right list the linear functions and rotations, and the quadratic functions, that transform like each row.

See it inAnatomy aboveWater

Representation Γ

#

A set of matrices, one per symmetry operation, that multiply together in the same way as the operations do. You build one by choosing a basis and writing down what each operation does to it. For most chemistry only the characters (the traces of the matrices) are needed. Representations are written Γ, capital gamma.

See it inWater

Basis

#

The objects (vectors, functions, orbitals, bond stretches) whose behaviour under the symmetry operations generates a representation. Different bases give different representations of the same group.

ExampleWater's 9 x, y, z displacement vectors are the basis for Γ3N. Its two O–H bond stretches are the basis for a two-dimensional representation that reduces to A1 + B1 (molecule in the xz plane).

See it inWater

Reducible representation

#

A representation that a change of basis can break into smaller ones. Its characters are a sum of rows of the character table, and the reduction formula finds which rows and how many of each. Γ3N is reducible.

ExampleFor water in the xz plane, Γ3N has characters 9, −1, 3, 1 and reduces to 3A1 + A2 + 3B1 + 2B2.

See it inWater

Irreducible representation (irrep)

#

A representation that cannot be reduced any further. Each one is a row of the character table. Every vibration, orbital and electronic state of a molecule belongs to one of them. A group has as many irreducible representations as it has classes.

ExampleC3v has three: A1 and A2 (one-dimensional) and E (two-dimensional).

See it inAmmonia

Character χ

#

The trace (sum of the diagonal elements) of the matrix that represents a symmetry operation. Characters do not change if you switch to an equivalent basis, and they are the same for every operation in a class. In a one-dimensional representation, χ = +1 means the basis function is unchanged by the operation and χ = −1 means it changes sign.

ExampleFor Γ3N, χ(R) = (number of unmoved atoms) × (contribution per unmoved atom).

See it inAnatomy aboveWater

Dimension & degeneracy

#

The size of the matrices in a representation, read off as χ(E). One-dimensional irreducible representations are A or B, two-dimensional ones E, and three-dimensional ones T. Functions or vibrations in an E or T representation are degenerate. An E vibration is a degenerate pair: two modes with the same frequency that give one band. In the degrees-of-freedom check, each E counts as 2 and each T as 3.

ExampleAmmonia's Γvib = 2A1 + 2E has six vibrations but only four different frequencies.

See it inAmmoniaBenzeneAllene

Totally symmetric representation

#

The irreducible representation with χ = +1 under every operation. It is always the first row of the table: A1, A1g, A′, Ag, A, Σ+ or Σg+, depending on the group. An integral over all space can be non-zero only if its integrand contains it, which is where selection rules come from. Totally symmetric vibrations are always Raman active, because x2 + y2 + z2 is totally symmetric in every group.

ExampleWater's symmetric stretch and bend are both A1.

See it inWater

Mulliken symbols

#

The labels for irreducible representations:

  • A / B: one-dimensional; symmetric (χ = +1) / antisymmetric (χ = −1) under rotation about the principal axis.
  • E: two-dimensional. T (sometimes F): three-dimensional.
  • Subscript 1 / 2: symmetric / antisymmetric under a C2 perpendicular to the principal axis or, if there is none, under a σv.
  • g / u (German gerade, even / ungerade, odd): symmetric / antisymmetric under inversion.
  • ′ / ″: symmetric / antisymmetric under σh.

Lower-case letters (a1, e, t2) are often used for orbitals.

ExampleWhich C2v plane is called xz is a convention, so water's antisymmetric stretch is B1 in some books and B2 in others.

See it inWaterBenzeneDioxygen

A and B labels

#

Both mark a one-dimensional representation. A: the character under rotation about the principal axis is +1, so the function is unchanged by the turn. B: the character is −1, so the turn reverses its sign.

D2h has three perpendicular C2 axes and none outranks the others, so there A means +1 under all three, and B1, B2, B3 are +1 under just one of them: C2(z), C2(y) or C2(x).

ExampleWater's symmetric stretch is A1 and its antisymmetric stretch is a B. In O2, the σg orbitals are Ag and the bonding π orbital along x is B3u.

See it inDioxygenWater

g and u labels gerade, ungerade

#

Only groups with an inversion centre use them. g (German gerade, even): χ(i) = +1, so inversion leaves the function unchanged. u (ungerade, odd): χ(i) = −1, so inversion reverses its sign.

ExampleIn O2, σ bonding orbitals are g but π bonding orbitals are u, so g does not mean "bonding". x, y, z are always u and quadratic functions always g, which is the root of the rule of mutual exclusion.

See it inDioxygenCarbon dioxide

Orthogonality & the great orthogonality theorem

#

Treat each row of the character table as a list of numbers and weight each column by its class size g. Two different irreducible representations are orthogonal: Σ g(R) χi(R) χj(R) = 0. A row paired with itself gives h. These results come from the great orthogonality theorem. The same theorem shows that the number of irreducible representations equals the number of classes, and that the squares of their dimensions add up to h. Orthogonality is why the reduction formula works.

ExampleIn C3v, A1 with E gives (1)(1)(2) + (2)(1)(−1) + (3)(1)(0) = 0.

See it inOrthogonality check above

Reduction formula

#
ni = 1h ΣR g(R) · χ(R) · χi(R)

This gives how many times irreducible representation i appears in a reducible representation Γ. Multiply Γ's characters by row i's characters column by column, weight each column by its class size g, add, and divide by the order h. The answer must be a whole number, zero or more. A fraction means an arithmetic slip.

ExampleAmmonia's Γ3N is 12, 0, 2, so n(A1) = (1/6)[(1)(12)(1) + (2)(0)(1) + (3)(2)(1)] = 18/6 = 3.

See it inWaterAmmonia

Direct sum

#

A reducible representation written as a sum of irreducible ones, such as Γ3N = 3A1 + A2 + 3B1 + 2B2 (some books write ⊕). The coefficients are the ni from the reduction formula, and the characters add column by column. Do not confuse it with the direct product.

See it inWater

Direct product

#

The representation of a product of two functions. Its characters are the products of the two sets of characters, column by column, and you reduce it if it is reducible. Direct products are used in selection rules and to find the symmetry of overtone and combination bands.

ExampleIn C2v, B1 × B2 = A2. In C3v, E × E has characters 4, 1, 0, which reduce to A1 + A2 + E. Any representation times the totally symmetric one is unchanged, and any one-dimensional representation times itself is totally symmetric.

Correlation table

#

A table showing what each irreducible representation of a group becomes (correlates with) in one of its subgroups. A degenerate representation can split into several one-dimensional ones. Correlation tables are used to handle the infinite groups and to predict how bands split when a molecule is substituted or distorted.

ExampleCO2, from D∞h to D2h with z along the molecule: Σg+ → Ag, Σu+ → B1u, Πu → B2u + B3u.

See it inCarbon dioxide

Projection operator

#
P(i)f ∝ ΣR χi(R) · R f

A recipe that takes any starting function f (one bond stretch, one atom's displacement, one atomic orbital) and keeps only the part that belongs to irreducible representation i. Apply every operation R to f (all of them, not one per class), multiply by χi(R), and add. The result, if it is not zero, is a symmetry-adapted linear combination (SALC). The full prefactor (di/h) only sets the scale and is often dropped.

ExampleStart from one O–H stretch r1 of water. Projecting onto A1 gives r1 + r2, the symmetric stretch. Projecting onto B1 (molecule in xz) gives r1 − r2, the antisymmetric stretch.

See it inDioxygenBenzene

Vibrations

Degrees of freedom 3N, 3N − 6, 3N − 5

#

Locating N atoms takes 3N coordinates, so a molecule has 3N degrees of freedom. Three are translations and three are rotations (two for a linear molecule). That leaves 3N − 6 vibrations for a nonlinear molecule and 3N − 5 for a linear one.

Degrees-of-freedom check: after reducing Γ3N, add up (number of times each irreducible representation appears) × (its dimension). The total must equal 3N.

ExampleWater: 3(3) − 6 = 3. CO2: 3(3) − 5 = 4. Ammonia: 6. Benzene: 30. For ammonia, 3A1 + A2 + 4E gives 3 + 1 + 4 × 2 = 12 = 3N.

See it inWaterCarbon dioxide

Γ3N

#

The reducible representation whose basis is the 3N Cartesian displacement vectors: an x, y and z arrow on every atom. It contains every motion of the molecule, including translations, rotations and vibrations. Shortcut: χ(R) = (number of unmoved atoms) × (contribution per unmoved atom).

ExampleWater (molecule in xz): 9, −1, 3, 1. Ammonia: 12, 0, 2.

See it inWaterAmmonia

Unmoved atoms & contribution per atom

#

An atom is unmoved by an operation if it stays in its own place, not just swapped with an identical atom. Only unmoved atoms add to χ(Γ3N), because the vectors of an atom that moves land off the diagonal of the matrix. Each unmoved atom adds the trace of the 3 × 3 matrix that the operation applies to (x, y, z):

Translations & rotations Γtrans, Γrot

#

Motions of the whole molecule that do not change its shape. Translations transform like x, y, z and rotations like Rx, Ry, Rz, so you can read their irreducible representations straight off the character table. Subtract them from Γ3N to get Γvib. A linear molecule has only two rotations, because turning about its own axis moves no nuclei.

ExampleWater (molecule in xz): Γtrans = A1 + B1 + B2 and Γrot = A2 + B1 + B2.

See it inWaterCarbon dioxide

Γvib

#

Γvib = Γ3N − Γtrans − Γrot: the representation of the vibrations alone. Each one-dimensional irreducible representation in it is one normal mode, each E is a degenerate pair, and each T a degenerate set of three.

ExampleWater: 2A1 + B1 (molecule in xz; 2A1 + B2 in yz). Ammonia: 2A1 + 2E. CO2: Σg+ + Σu+ + Πu.

See it inWaterAmmoniaCarbon dioxide

Normal mode

#

A collective vibration in which every atom moves at the same frequency and in phase, so all atoms pass through equilibrium together. In the harmonic approximation the normal modes are independent, and any vibration of the molecule is a combination of them. Each normal mode, or degenerate set, belongs to one irreducible representation. Modes are numbered ν1, ν2, … by symmetry species first, then by decreasing wavenumber.

ExampleWater, gas phase: ν1 symmetric stretch, 3657 cm−1; ν2 bend, 1595 cm−1; ν3 antisymmetric stretch, 3756 cm−1.

See it inWaterBenzene

Symmetry species

#

The irreducible representation that a normal mode (or orbital, or state) belongs to, written as its Mulliken symbol. To test a mode, apply each operation to its displacement arrows. χ = +1 if the pattern maps onto itself and −1 if every arrow reverses.

ExampleWater's antisymmetric stretch changes sign under C2, so it is a B species.

See it inWater

Fundamental

#

A transition from the vibrational ground state (v = 0 in every mode) to v = 1 in one normal mode. Fundamentals give the strongest vibrational bands, and the IR and Raman rules read from the character table apply to them. The excited state has the symmetry species of the mode.

See it inWater

Overtone & combination bands

#

An overtone excites one mode by two or more quanta (v = 0 → 2 appears near twice the fundamental wavenumber, a little lower because of anharmonicity). A combination band excites two different modes at once and appears near νa + νb. Both are weak, because only anharmonicity allows them. Their symmetry is the direct product of the species involved, so the first overtone of a non-degenerate mode is always totally symmetric. They can reveal silent modes.

See it inBenzene

Fermi resonance

#

When an overtone or combination level has nearly the same energy as a fundamental and the same symmetry species, the two states mix. They push apart and share intensity, so two bands of similar strength appear where one fundamental was expected.

ExampleIn the Raman spectrum of CO2, the symmetric stretch ν1 (Σg+) mixes with 2ν2, the overtone of the 667 cm−1 bend, which has a Σg+ component. The result is a pair of bands at 1285 and 1388 cm−1.

Spectroscopy

Selection rule & transition integral

#

The intensity of a transition from state ψi to ψf caused by an operator Ô (the dipole moment μ for IR, the polarizability α for Raman) is proportional to |⟨ψf|Ô|ψi⟩|2. The integral can be non-zero only if the direct product Γf × ΓÔ × Γi contains the totally symmetric representation. For a fundamental the ground state is totally symmetric, so the mode must have the same species as a component of Ô. Symmetry tells you whether a band is allowed, not how strong it is.

See it inWater

IR active

#

A vibration absorbs infrared light only if it changes the molecule's dipole moment μ. The components μx, μy, μz transform like x, y, z, so a fundamental is IR active if its row of the character table lists x, y or z. The function listed tells you the direction in which the dipole oscillates.

ExampleAll three water fundamentals are IR active. The symmetric stretch of CO2 (Σg+) is not.

See it inWaterCarbon dioxide

Polarizability α

#

How easily an electric field distorts the electron cloud. The induced dipole is μind = αE. α is a symmetric 3 × 3 tensor whose components αxx, αxy, … transform like the quadratic functions x2, xy, …. A vibration that makes α oscillate can Raman scatter.

See it inWater

Raman active

#

In Raman scattering, light is scattered with its frequency shifted by a vibrational frequency. A vibration is Raman active if it changes the polarizability, which means its row of the character table lists a quadratic function (x2, xy, x2 − y2, …). Totally symmetric modes are always Raman active and give polarized bands.

ExampleThe symmetric stretch of CO2 is Raman active but IR inactive.

See it inWaterCarbon dioxideBenzene

Rule of mutual exclusion

#

In a molecule with an inversion centre, no vibration is both IR and Raman active. x, y and z change sign under i (they are u), while quadratic functions do not (they are g). So IR-active modes are u and Raman-active modes are g. A mode can still be neither (silent). If many bands appear at the same wavenumber in both spectra, the molecule probably has no inversion centre.

ExampleCO2: the symmetric stretch is Raman only, while the antisymmetric stretch and the bend are IR only.

See it inCarbon dioxideBenzene

Silent mode

#

A normal mode whose fundamental is neither IR nor Raman active, because its row lists neither x, y, z nor a quadratic function. Silent modes still exist. They can be found through combination bands, inelastic neutron scattering, or by lowering the symmetry (for example, by isotopic substitution).

ExampleAn A2 vibration in C3v would be silent. Benzene has 20 distinct fundamental frequencies: 4 IR active, 7 Raman active and 9 silent.

See it inBenzene

Orbitals & bonding

Molecular orbital & LCAO

#

A molecular orbital (MO) spreads over the whole molecule. In the LCAO approximation (linear combination of atomic orbitals) each MO is a sum of atomic orbitals (AOs) with coefficients. Orbitals are conserved: n AOs always give n MOs. AOs mix only if they have the same symmetry, overlap well and have similar energies.

ExampleThe 8 valence AOs of O2 (2s, 2px, 2py, 2pz on each atom) give 8 valence MOs.

See it inDioxygen

SALC symmetry-adapted linear combination

#

A combination of equivalent orbitals (or other basis functions) that transforms as exactly one irreducible representation. SALCs are built with the projection operator, and a reducible representation predicts which symmetries appear. Only SALCs of the same symmetry can combine into a molecular orbital.

ExampleIn O2, with both 2pz orbitals pointing along +z: 2pz(A) − 2pz(B) is Ag (σg) and 2pz(A) + 2pz(B) is B1u (σu).

See it inDioxygen

Bonding & antibonding orbitals

#

Where lobes of the same sign (phase) overlap, the waves add, electron density builds up between the nuclei, and the MO is bonding: lower in energy than the AOs. Where lobes of opposite sign overlap, a nodal plane cuts the bond and the MO is antibonding, marked *: higher in energy. A nonbonding orbital has no net overlap and keeps roughly its AO energy.

ExampleO2: 1πu is bonding and 1πg* antibonding.

See it inDioxygen

σ, π and δ orbitals

#

Labels for the orbitals of a linear molecule, by how many nodal planes contain the bond axis: σ none, π one, δ two. They are the lower-case forms of the D∞h and C∞v labels Σ, Π and Δ. A σ orbital is unchanged by any rotation about the bond; π and δ orbitals come in degenerate pairs.

ExampleIn O2, 3σg comes from end-on 2pz overlap and 1πu from side-on 2px and 2py overlap.

See it inDioxygen

Bond order

#
bond order = ½ (bonding electrons − antibonding electrons)

A higher bond order means a shorter, stronger bond. Unpaired electrons in the MO diagram make a molecule paramagnetic; if every electron is paired it is diamagnetic.

ExampleO2+ 2.5, O2 2 (two unpaired electrons, paramagnetic), O2− 1.5, O22− 1.

See it inDioxygen

s–p mixing

#

In a homonuclear diatomic, the SALCs from 2s and from 2pz share symmetries (σg with σg, σu with σu), so they can mix. The mixing pushes the lower orbital of each pair down and the upper one up. It is strong when the 2s–2p gap is small (B2, C2, N2), where it lifts σg(2p) above πu, and weak in O2 and F2.

See it inDioxygen
