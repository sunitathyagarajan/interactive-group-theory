Finding a molecule's point group

← All lessonsGlossary

A molecule's point group is the set of symmetry operations that leave it looking unchanged. You can find it with a short series of yes/no questions, the flowchart below. Practise on 3D models, where each answer is checked and the symmetry elements can be drawn for you, or walk through the questions for a molecule you have on paper.

Tips

Finding the principal axis

It is the highest-order rotation axis. For a flat ring it is perpendicular to the ring through its centre; for a chain it usually runs along the backbone (C–C in ethane, C=C=C in allene); for a pyramid it runs through the apex. If several C2 axes tie, choose the unique one, such as the C2 that is also an S4 in allene. In D2 and D2h any of the three works.

σv or σd?

Both contain the principal axis. In a D group a σd bisects the angle between two neighbouring C2′ axes, while a σv contains one. A D-group molecule whose vertical planes contain the C2′ axes always has σh as well, so the chart has already sent it to Dnh. C groups have no C2′ axes, and every vertical plane is called σv.

Why S2n is easy to miss

An S2n rotates by 360°/2n and then reflects through the plane perpendicular to the axis. Neither half is a symmetry operation on its own, so you cannot spot it by looking for an axis or a mirror. Check for it whenever a molecule reaches the end of the C branch, especially twisted molecules with no mirror planes (try tetramethylcyclooctatetraene).

Linear molecules: a shortcut

There are only two possibilities. If the molecule is the same at both ends (O=C=O, H–C≡C–H, N2) it has a centre of inversion and is D∞h. If the ends differ (HCN, CO, OCS) it is C∞v. You can skip the rest of the chart.

High symmetry means a regular solid

Td, Oh and Ih are the regular tetrahedron, octahedron (or cube) and icosahedron (or dodecahedron), with identical atoms at every corner. CH3Cl is tetrahedral in shape but only C3v.
